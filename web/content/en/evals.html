Round three: the whole-series outline

Everyone gets the same premise card: a coroner whose fiancé broke off their engagement goes to his wedding and sees at a glance that the groom is an impostor, because the real one lay on her autopsy table three years ago. The brief asks for the whole-series outline: how all 60 episodes unfold, to keep the later episode outlines and scripts on track. It is to become a vertical short drama of 60 episodes, 1–2 minutes each, made entirely by AI; only the outline is wanted, within 3,000 characters. All 20 skills tested in rounds one and two took part, plus a control with no skill, all on Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies. Since everyone wrote the same story, the only difference is how it was spread over 60 episodes.

Reviewers could not see who wrote which outline. All 21 entries were compared in pairs, run n against run n, with the order swapped each time; an entry only wins if it wins both orders. A win scores 1 point and a tie 0.5; each entry has 60 comparisons, so 60 is the maximum. The reviewer prompt states only the goal: if this outline is written and filmed, are viewers more likely to keep watching episode after episode to the end. Filming cost and difficulty don't count, no scoring dimensions are listed, and how to judge is up to the reviewer. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts. The two reviewers disagreed more than in round two, so the findings below cover only what both agree on. They are the AI reviewers' views, not what viewers chose.

Findings

  1. The AI reviewers found four skills better than no skill. hongguo-shortdrama-screenwriting, emma-screenwriting-skills, Tomsawyerhu/Chinese-WebNovel-Skill and short-drama-factory are in both reviewers' top five, and both rank them above the control. What both reviewers saw in all four is the 60 episodes broken into units they judged ready to write from. hongguo and short-drama-factory run a round every three to five episodes, each spelling out what evidence comes out, how the opponent hits back and how the round ends. Chinese-WebNovel-Skill pins the big cliffhangers to specific episodes and requires each one to "change the game, not just leave a mystery". emma keeps a "who knows what" table fixing the episode in which the audience and the heroine each learn each thing. The two reviewers picked different winners: Opus chose hongguo, GPT Chinese-WebNovel-Skill.
  2. Both reviewers ranked six skills below the control. They are Distilled-Novel-Toolbox, webnovel-writer, bookflow-skill, chinese-longnovel-skill, qianqianwriting and shuohao-skills. They mostly lost points in two places: the outline stops at sections or acts without saying what drives each episode inside them, or there is a long slump, or key evidence arrives without being set up. Most of them write a top-level series bible, and their strengths lie elsewhere, such as patching the premise, locking the order of reveals or pinning payoffs to episodes. They are useful when a screenwriter or AI fills in episode by episode afterwards; see "Good for" below.
  3. On the other ten, the reviewers disagree. The widest splits are ai-short-drama (Opus 13th, GPT 3rd), screenwriting-skills (Opus 4th, GPT 11th) and manju-laoli-skill (Opus 18th, GPT 7th). Their reasons show one difference in taste. Opus often counts rule tables, setup tables and a trump card held back for the finale as strengths. GPT more often calls these promises that never land, or answers handed over by coincidence, and cares more about whether answers arrive early and whether the heroine earns her comebacks herself.
  4. Where both reviewers took points off, it was mostly at traps in the premise card itself. The card says the heroine spots at the wedding that the groom is an impostor, yet many outlines spend another ten or twenty episodes proving what viewers already know. The unidentified man she autopsied three years earlier was in fact her fiancé, and the card doesn't say why she failed to recognize him then; outlines that filled that gap gained points, and those that didn't lost them. Key evidence turning up too conveniently (a memory card hidden in an old watch, a recording on a repaired phone) cost points to more than half of the 21 entries. Almost every outline puts the same twist around episode 30, the control included, so that is not something the skills added.
  5. Which skill to pick depends on the step you are stuck on. Of the four skills both reviewers found beating the control in all three script runs in round one, only emma made both reviewers' top five this round. webnovel-writer, top three for premises in round two, is below the control with both reviewers here. Two have stayed near the top across rounds: emma (scripts in round one, outlines here) and Chinese-WebNovel-Skill (premises in round two, outlines here).
  6. Some skills score about the same every run; others swing. For hongguo, emma and ai-short-drama, the highest and lowest of the three runs are within 5.5 points of each other with both reviewers (out of 20 per run). shanyin swings most: above 17 with both reviewers in run 1, under 5 in run 2.

Round two: choosing a premise

The brief asks for the premise of a new revenge-fantasy story (the "satisfying payback" genre big on Chinese web novel sites and vertical drama apps) that could later become a web novel or a vertical short drama. Only the premise: a one-line pitch and a premise note. No genre, length or platform. The entries were 11 skills, picked by stars from those in the short drama and novel lists that can come up with a premise from scratch, plus a control with no skill, all on Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies. When a skill offered several options, it was told to pick the one it recommends most.

Reviewers could not see who wrote which premise. Each premise got a story card, to see how alike they are. Then all 12 entries were compared in pairs, run n against run n, with the order swapped each time; an entry only wins if it wins both orders. A win scores 1 point and a tie 0.5, each entry has 33 comparisons, and entries are ranked by points. Only the final recommended premise counts, judged on five things: the pitch, freshness, conflict and payoffs, staying power, and how it plays on screen. Length and market analysis earn nothing. Reviewers judge as if AI will make the whole thing: effects, powers, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts lose no points, and big spectacle earns none. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts.

Round one: an open brief

The brief says only "write a vertical short drama", plus the length (60 episodes of 1–2 minutes) and three deliverables: a character bible, a 60-episode outline with paywall points, and full scripts for episodes 1–3. No genre, characters or plot. Every run used Claude Code with claude-opus-5-5 at high effort and word-for-word identical prompts and replies, so the only variable is which skill was installed. Scripts that ship with a skill could run inside a system sandbox.

The review had two parts, and neither could see which skill wrote what. First, each draft got a story card, to see how alike the stories are. Then run n of each skill was compared with run n of the control, once for the scripts and once for the characters and outline. Each comparison was repeated with the order swapped, and a skill only counts as winning if it won both times. The main reviewer was claude-opus-5-5; GPT (gpt-6-astra) then re-judged everything with word-for-word identical prompts, and results where the two reviewers disagree are marked as disputed. Reviewers judge as if AI will make the whole thing: effects, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts lose no points, and big spectacle earns none.

Findings

  1. Scripts: four skills beat the control in all three runs, and both reviewers agree. drama-skills, Shanyin Screenwriting Master, screenwriting-skills and Emma won all three runs under both Opus and GPT. They won on dialogue, AI tells and how well the script plays on screen: short lines, characters who sound different, emotion carried by actions and objects, and episodes that fit in one to two minutes. On the other six, the reviewers split: Opus found none of them beating the control in most runs, while GPT found all of them did except short-drama-factory.
  2. Characters and outlines: five skills won all three runs, and both reviewers agree. The same four plus ai-short-drama. Skills had no edge on paywall points, and all 33 drafts put the first paywall at episode 10. Both reviewers gave shuohao two losses and a tie; the other four are disputed.
  3. Skills do change which story gets written. The 33 dramas fell into 21 story types. Two runs of the same skill landed in the same type 12% of the time; two runs of different skills, only 2.4%. hongguo, for example, wrote a daughter-in-law settling accounts with her in-laws all three times, and ai-short-drama wrote two dramas called The Old Brine.

  5. Most skills do not ask you to pick a direction first. Across 33 runs, only a few runs of manju-laoli, Shanyin and shuohao stopped to offer several story options.
  6. Everything needs cleanup before it goes to AI production. Every skill's drafts carry IDs, runtime estimates, checkpoints or self-check tables mixed into the text.

How we test

  1. The same input, as little as possible: the brief gives only the length and deliverables, with no genre, characters or plot, and every skill in the round shares it. Round three compares outlines, so everyone gets the same premise card.
  2. The skill is the only variable: the same agent, model, reasoning effort and tool permissions, with versions recorded. The control just has no skill.
  3. Pinned skill versions: the commit of each repo at test time is recorded.
  4. Each skill's own process: the run opens by calling the entry skill. When a skill stops to ask, it gets the same fixed reply, and everything else follows the skill's own recommendation. If it offered several genres or story options, in round one run n picked option n, so the three runs would tell different stories; from round two on it picks the option it recommends most.
  5. Skill scripts can run: commands run in a system sandbox with no network and no view of other entries' output.
  6. Three runs each, all kept: no cherry-picking.
  7. Blind review: reviewers do not know which skill wrote a draft, and every comparison is run in both orders.
  8. Judged as AI-made film: reviewers assume the draft goes to AI to be made start to finish, with no actors or sets. Effects, powers, big set pieces, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts neither lose nor earn points; what counts is whether viewers would keep watching. Round three's reviewer prompt states only the goal and that filming cost and difficulty don't count, without listing these one by one.

Round two compares premises on five things: the pitch (does the one-liner make you want it now), freshness (is it a familiar formula with new names), conflict and payoffs (what the hero wants, who stands in the way, can the payoffs keep coming), staying power (does it hold up for the length it plans), and on screen (who the audience rides with and whether that person has a face that can act; whose face each payoff lands on; whether key emotions lean on inner monologue).

Round three compares whole-series outlines and lists no dimensions. The reviewers are only told the goal: if the outline is written and filmed, are viewers more likely to keep watching episode after episode to the end.

Reviews give observations and examples. Round one has no overall scores; from round two on, everyone is compared with everyone and ranked by points. The runner is evals/run.py and the reviewer is evals/judge.py, so anyone can reproduce the method.
