Install a screenwriting skill that won our blind test before you ask the agent to write; the result plays better on screen than asking the model directly. Three steps still need you: picking the direction, renaming characters and stripping metadata. This advice comes from round one of our tests: 10 screenwriting skills and a no-skill control, same model, same brief, three runs each, 33 dramas in all, compared blind by two AI reviewers.

1. Install a skill that won the test

A skill is a packaged writing method and set of templates that an agent loads; see what is a short drama skill. Installing one is not enough on its own: only 4 of the 10 beat the control in all three runs with both reviewers agreeing, and the reviewers split sharply on the other six.

  • Won all three runs on both the script and the characters and outline: drama-skills, Shanyin Screenwriting Master, screenwriting-skills and Emma.
  • Won all three runs on characters and outline: ai-short-drama.

See the install guide; the quickest way is npx skills add owner/repo. These results come from claude-opus-5-5 only and may differ on other models. The brief was in Chinese, so they describe Chinese-language scripts.

2. Give only the length and the deliverables

The brief in the test was a few lines, with no genre, characters or plot, and these skills still finished the job:

Write a vertical short drama.
- Length: 60 episodes, 1–2 minutes each
- Deliverables (as files in the current folder):
  1. Bios of the main characters
  2. A 60-episode outline with the paywall points marked
  3. Full scripts for episodes 1–3

Save it as premise.md and tell the agent "I want to write a short drama; the brief is in premise.md." If you already have a genre or characters in mind, add them. In the test, one run from brief to all three deliverables took 9 to 35 minutes.

3. Ask for options first, then choose

Across 33 runs, only a few runs of manju-laoli, Shanyin and shuohao stopped to offer several story options; the rest picked one and wrote it to the end. Skills also pull stories in a fixed direction: two runs of the same skill landed in the same story type 12% of the time, two runs of different skills only 2.4%. hongguo, for example, wrote a daughter-in-law settling accounts with her in-laws all three times.

So start with: "First give me three story options with different genres and different leads, each with a one-line pitch, and wait for me to choose." If a skill keeps writing the same kind of story, try another one.

4. Replace the model's default names and premises

The model's habits survive whichever skill you install: 15 of the 33 drafts have a character named Xiaoman, and 10 heroines have the surname Shen. zenstory and shuohao each wrote a drama called The Appraiser whose heroine is chief appraiser at the same fictional auction house. Many people use the same model, so if you keep the defaults, your drama is likely to share names and premises with someone else's.

Search the finished draft for every name and replace them with your own. Then ask of the opening and the premise: with the names changed, is this just the most common kind of story?

5. Check every episode for how it plays on screen

The reviewers assumed the script goes to AI for the whole production: no actors or sets, with picture, sound and lip sync all generated by video models. The winning skills won on the points below, so check for them:

  • Each episode fits in 1–2 minutes, estimated from the action and dialogue. The control lost by cramming too much into each episode, often running well past two minutes.
  • Emotion shows on faces, in actions and in objects, not in inner monologue or narration. In one screenwriting-skills draft, a mute wife serves soup to her husband's pregnant mistress on their wedding anniversary, and "the surface of the soup doesn't so much as ripple." The control often explained key deductions in voice-over.
  • Lines are short and each character sounds different. Cut stock phrases such as "her smile didn't reach her eyes" or "his voice was cold as ice."

Effects, superpowers, spectacle, crowd scenes, many locations and large casts are not weaknesses: when AI makes the film, they cost nothing extra. Spectacle is not a strength in itself either; it counts only when it lands on the characters and the payoffs.

6. Check the episode endings and paywall points yourself

Skills showed no clear edge here. On paywall points, the main reviewer's 60 judgments went to the control 34 times and to a skill only 11 times. All 33 drafts put the first paywall at episode 10, which is both industry practice and the model's default. On episode-ending hooks the reviewers disagreed: Opus found the control stronger, GPT found the skills stronger.

So read the last scene of every episode on its own and ask whether you would tap through to the next one. The paywall episode has to end hard. At the control's first paywall, in episode 10, the heroine has just won a round when a text arrives: "Dad will stop breathing at three tonight. It won't be an accident."

7. Strip the metadata before AI production

Every skill's drafts carry IDs, runtime estimates, checkpoints, self-check tables and template tags, such as "estimated 90.8 seconds", "Continuation Capsule" or a "settlement for this episode" header. In 110 of its 120 comparisons, the main reviewer said these had to be removed before the script could be used. Ask the agent to save a clean copy with only scenes, action and dialogue, then take that to storyboarding and video prompts.

What these results do and do not show

  • Only one model was tested, claude-opus-5-5; results may differ on others.
  • The reviewers are AI. When GPT re-judged with word-for-word identical prompts, 39 of the 60 pairings came out the same, so step 1 only names skills both reviewers agree on.
  • The test compared scripts; none has been made into a film yet.
